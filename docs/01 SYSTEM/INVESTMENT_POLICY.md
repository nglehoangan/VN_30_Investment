## 1. Purpose

This Investment Policy defines the highest-level rules governing the VN30 Value Investing OS. Its purpose is to ensure that portfolio decisions remain consistent with a long-term value-investing discipline, capital preservation, portfolio risk control, and repeatable decision-making.

This document governs what the system may invest in, the investment horizon, capital deployment, portfolio construction principles, risk boundaries, DCA behavior, review discipline, and exceptions.

Detailed security scoring, Buy/Hold/Sell rules, valuation models, entry rules, and workflow mechanics will be defined in later Milestone 1 documents and must remain consistent with this policy.

## 2. Investment Objective

### 2.1 Primary objective

Compound portfolio capital over the long term by investing in businesses within the VN30 that satisfy minimum investability standards and offer attractive expected long-term returns relative to risk and available alternatives.

Improvement in business performance may strengthen an investment case, but an “improving” company does not bypass minimum standards for business viability, financial health, governance, and risk.

### 2.2 Target return

The long-term aspirational return target is:

- **15%–20% portfolio total-return CAGR over a full investment cycle.**

Total return includes capital appreciation and dividends and should be evaluated net of transaction costs and applicable taxes where measurable.

The target should be assessed over a multi-year horizon, with **5 years or more** preferred for meaningful evaluation rather than a single calendar year.

This is a target, not a guaranteed return and not a requirement that must be achieved every calendar year.

The system should also compare results with an appropriate **VN30 total-return benchmark** to evaluate capital-allocation quality, but benchmark-relative performance does not replace the absolute long-term return objective.

Benchmark comparison is for performance evaluation and learning only. It does **not** create a requirement to replicate VN30 constituent weights, minimize tracking error, or hold a security solely because it has a large benchmark weight.

The system must not increase risk, lower quality standards, use leverage, or force trades merely to pursue the target return.

### 2.3 Capital preservation

Capital preservation is a core objective and has priority over pursuit of the aspirational return target when the two conflict.

The governing objective hierarchy is:

1. **Mandate compliance.**
2. **Avoid unacceptable permanent-capital-loss risk.**
3. **Preserve portfolio resilience and liquidity.**
4. **Optimize long-term risk-adjusted compounding among opportunities that satisfy the first three objectives.**

The 15%–20% return target must never be used to justify violating this hierarchy.

The system should prioritize avoiding permanent capital loss caused by:

- weak or deteriorating businesses;
- excessive leverage;
- governance problems;
- structurally declining economics;
- overvaluation;
- thesis errors;
- excessive portfolio concentration;
- behavioral mistakes.

Short-term price volatility alone is not considered permanent capital loss.

## 3. Investment Universe

### 3.1 Eligible securities

The portfolio may invest only in equities that are current constituents of the **VN30 Index**.

No security outside the VN30 may be newly purchased under this mandate.

### 3.2 VN30 constituent changes

When the VN30 composition changes:

1. New constituents become eligible for research and future investment.
2. Removed constituents become ineligible for new purchases.
3. Existing holdings removed from the VN30 become **Legacy Holdings** and must not receive additional capital.
4. A Legacy Holding may be retained temporarily only to support an orderly exit based on valuation, liquidity, tax/fees where relevant, thesis quality, and opportunity cost.
5. Each Legacy Holding must have a documented exit plan and remain under periodic review until fully exited.

The system must not mechanically exchange one stock for another solely because index membership changed, but it must not allow a Legacy Holding to remain indefinitely as a de facto investment outside the VN30 mandate.

### 3.3 Number of holdings

The portfolio may eventually own many or all VN30 constituents, but **ownership is never required simply because a company belongs to the VN30**.

Each holding must independently pass the applicable quality, valuation, risk, and portfolio-allocation standards.

Cash is preferable to owning a low-quality or unattractively valued VN30 security.

## 4. Investment Philosophy

### 4.1 Core style

The primary investment philosophy is **Value Investing**.

Value is defined as purchasing an ownership interest in a business when the relationship between:

- business quality;
- financial strength;
- durable earnings/cash-flow potential;
- growth prospects;
- risk;
- and market price

provides a sufficiently attractive expected long-term return.

A low valuation multiple alone does not establish value.

### 4.2 Decision sequence

Before the canonical analysis begins, every security must pass a **Stage 0 — Eligibility / Investability / Veto Screen**. Stage 0 determines whether the security is eligible for analysis as a positive capital-allocation candidate and whether any policy-level condition prevents investment before valuation work can influence judgment.

Stage 0 must screen at minimum for:

- VN30 mandate eligibility;
- basic business viability;
- acceptable financial health sufficient for further underwriting;
- acceptable governance and reporting reliability;
- availability of sufficient evidence to form a defensible thesis;
- and absence of applicable hard veto conditions defined by `RISK_POLICY.md`.

If Stage 0 fails because of a hard veto or mandate violation, valuation attractiveness must not override the failure. Analysis may continue only when necessary to document an existing holding, exit decision, or risk event, and the reason must be explicit.

For securities that pass Stage 0, investment analysis should use the following sequence as the canonical framework:

1. Business Quality
2. Financial Health
3. Growth
4. Industry
5. Valuation
6. Full Risk Analysis
7. Market / Money Flow
8. Technical Entry
9. Existing Portfolio
10. Opportunity Cost
11. Final Decision

No Buy, Add, Reduce, or Sell decision may be based on a single metric in isolation.

A step may be marked **N/A** when it is not decision-relevant, provided the reason is explicit. For example, Technical Entry may be N/A for a thesis-break SELL decision. The sequence must not be applied mechanically when doing so would reduce decision quality.

### 4.3 Four concepts that must remain separate

The system must always distinguish:

- **Price** — what the market currently quotes.
- **Business quality** — the economic strength and durability of the enterprise.
- **Intrinsic value** — the system's estimate or range of reasonable economic value.
- **Investment thesis** — the explicit reasons the investment is expected to generate an attractive risk-adjusted return.

A falling price does not necessarily mean intrinsic value has increased.

A rising price does not necessarily mean business quality has improved.

### 4.4 Minimum investability floor

Before valuation can support a positive investment decision, the company must satisfy a minimum investability floor covering:

- basic business viability;
- acceptable financial health;
- acceptable governance and reporting reliability;
- absence of policy-level veto conditions;
- and sufficient evidence to form a defensible investment thesis.

A large valuation discount does not compensate for failure of a non-compensable investability requirement.

## 5. Investment Horizon

### 5.1 Target holding period

The normal target holding period is **5–10 years or longer** when:

- the business remains high quality;
- the investment thesis remains valid;
- capital allocation remains acceptable;
- valuation does not become excessively unattractive;
- and the holding remains competitive with alternative uses of capital.

### 5.2 No arbitrary profit-taking

A stock must not be sold merely because it has appreciated by 20% or any other arbitrary percentage.

A gain of approximately 20% is a **review trigger**, not an automatic sell signal.

The review should reassess:

- current valuation versus intrinsic value;
- expected forward return;
- position size;
- portfolio concentration;
- thesis quality;
- opportunity cost.

### 5.3 Price declines

A price decline is not, by itself, a reason to buy more.

Additional investment is permitted only when the thesis remains valid and the risk/reward has improved sufficiently after reassessment.

## 6. Capital Contributions and Cash Policy

### 6.1 Current contribution framework

The current planned contribution is approximately:

- **VND 5,000,000 per month.**

This amount may change over time and should be treated as a portfolio input rather than a permanent constitutional constant.

### 6.2 DCA is optional, not mandatory deployment

Monthly capital contributions do not create an obligation to invest immediately.

The system may:

- deploy all new cash;
- deploy only part of it;
- or deploy none of it

when sufficiently attractive opportunities are unavailable.

### 6.3 Cash accumulation

Cash may accumulate across multiple months to enable:

- compliance with minimum trading lots;
- better diversification;
- entry into higher-conviction opportunities;
- deployment when eligible opportunities become sufficiently attractive, including during broader market dislocations.

Cash is an acceptable temporary portfolio position and must not be treated as a failure to invest.

Cash accumulation should arise primarily from the absence of sufficiently attractive eligible opportunities, portfolio-risk constraints, liquidity or trading-lot constraints, or other explicit operational constraints. The system must not build unusually large cash positions primarily because of a speculative short-term forecast that the VN30 or market is about to decline.

Macro and market views may inform risk awareness and the pace of deployment, but they must not replace bottom-up valuation and opportunity-set analysis.

### 6.4 Minimum trade size

Under the current market implementation, each equity transaction should comply with the applicable board-lot rule, currently treated by this system as **100 shares** for planning purposes unless official exchange rules indicate otherwise.

The constitutional principle is that the system must comply with applicable trading-lot rules; the specific lot size is an operational parameter rather than a permanent policy constant.

The system must therefore evaluate affordability before recommending a purchase.

A theoretically attractive stock is not an actionable purchase when available cash is insufficient for the minimum lot.

## 7. DCA and Position Addition Policy

### 7.1 Conditions for adding to a position

An existing position may receive additional capital only when all of the following are satisfied:

1. The investment thesis remains valid or has improved.
2. Business quality has not deteriorated beyond acceptable limits.
3. Financial health remains acceptable.
4. Updated valuation offers an attractive expected return or margin of safety.
5. Key risks remain within policy tolerance.
6. The resulting position does not create unacceptable concentration risk.
7. The position remains attractive relative to other VN30 opportunities and cash.
8. The transaction satisfies the minimum lot and available-cash constraints.

Every Add / DCA decision must be treated as **fresh underwriting**. The system must update the thesis, key facts, valuation, major risks, and expected return using current information rather than simply relying on the original purchase case.

### 7.2 Prohibited averaging-down behavior

The system must not average down solely because:

- the market price declined;
- the position is showing a loss;
- the investor wishes to lower reported cost basis;
- the investor wants the price to recover to break-even.

Cost basis is not a valid anchor for intrinsic value.

### 7.3 Break-even is not an investment objective

The system must not buy additional shares primarily to enable an exit at 0% profit/loss.

Every additional purchase must be justified as a new capital-allocation decision on its own merits.

## 8. Portfolio Construction

### 8.1 Portfolio principle

Portfolio construction must balance:

- expected return;
- business quality;
- valuation;
- conviction;
- sector exposure;
- correlation;
- liquidity;
- position affordability;
- concentration risk;
- opportunity cost.

### 8.2 No requirement for equal weighting

The portfolio is not required to equal-weight all VN30 constituents.

Position size should reflect conviction, downside risk, valuation attractiveness, and portfolio diversification subject to limits defined in the Risk Policy.

### 8.3 No forced diversification

Diversification should reduce uncompensated risk, but the system must not buy inferior securities merely to increase the number of holdings.

### 8.4 No forced concentration

High conviction does not justify unlimited concentration.

Specific issuer, sector, and correlated-risk limits will be defined in `RISK_POLICY.md`.

### 8.5 Opportunity cost

Every new investment and every additional purchase must be compared with:

- other eligible VN30 opportunities;
- existing portfolio holdings;
- and holding cash.

The relevant question is not only "Is this stock attractive?" but also "Is this the best available use of incremental capital within the mandate?"

For portfolio switches, the replacement opportunity must be **materially superior**, not merely marginally higher-ranked. The decision must account for uncertainty, thesis confidence, transaction costs, taxes where relevant, liquidity, and the potential loss of future compounding from the existing holding.

As a governing principle, the hurdle to **switch out of a valid long-term holding should be higher than the hurdle to continue holding it**.

## 9. Drawdown and Risk Philosophy

### 9.1 Portfolio drawdown objective

A portfolio peak-to-trough drawdown of approximately **20%** is a **soft risk-tolerance and escalation threshold**, not a guaranteed maximum loss, stop-loss rule, or forced-deleveraging trigger.

Approaching or exceeding this level requires an elevated portfolio-risk review, but does not by itself justify selling high-quality holdings or interrupting a valid long-term thesis.

Equity markets may experience periods in which losses exceed this level despite prudent management, especially during broad systemic stress.

### 9.2 Drawdown must not dictate irrational trades

The system must not automatically:

- sell high-quality holdings merely because portfolio drawdown approaches 20%;
- average down solely to reduce percentage loss;
- or make speculative trades to recover losses.

Risk response must identify the **source of drawdown** before prescribing action. The review should distinguish among:

- broad market beta / systemic repricing;
- thesis deterioration;
- intrinsic-value impairment;
- valuation compression without thesis impairment;
- concentration;
- correlated exposures;
- balance-sheet risk;
- macro or industry shocks;
- liquidity effects;
- portfolio construction errors.

Market-driven drawdown and business-driven permanent impairment must not be treated as the same risk event.

### 9.3 Permanent-loss focus

Risk assessment should prioritize probability and magnitude of permanent capital impairment rather than short-term volatility alone.

However, volatility and drawdown remain relevant because they affect capital availability, concentration, behavioral discipline, and portfolio resilience.

## 10. Leverage and Financing

### 10.1 Margin

**Margin and other forms of investment leverage are prohibited.**

The portfolio must remain fully funded by contributed capital, realized proceeds, dividends, and cash already available to the portfolio.

### 10.2 No borrowing to meet DCA targets

The system must never recommend borrowing solely to maintain monthly contributions or exploit a perceived market opportunity.

## 11. Valuation Policy

### 11.1 Valuation is mandatory

No stock may receive a positive purchase recommendation without a valuation assessment.

### 11.2 Multiple methods

Valuation should use a **sector-appropriate primary method** that best reflects the economics of the business, supported by one or more useful independent cross-checks where appropriate.

Using additional methods is valuable only when those methods are economically relevant; the system must not average multiple weak or inappropriate models to create false confidence. Appropriate methods may include:

- normalized P/E;
- P/B where economically relevant;
- EV/EBITDA or EV/EBIT;
- dividend-based methods;
- free-cash-flow methods;
- residual-income methods;
- DCF;
- historical range comparison;
- peer comparison;
- sum-of-the-parts where appropriate.

The choice of method must reflect the economics of the business and sector.

### 11.3 Margin of safety

Purchase attractiveness should reflect uncertainty in intrinsic-value estimates.

For ordinary uncertainty, weaker predictability or business durability may justify a larger required margin of safety.

However, some risks are **non-compensable** and may constitute an investment **veto regardless of apparent valuation discount**. Examples may include severe governance or integrity concerns, materially unreliable financial reporting, unacceptable solvency risk, or other conditions that make intrinsic value insufficiently trustworthy.

`RISK_POLICY.md` is the authoritative owner of hard risk limits and investment veto definitions. `DECISION_FRAMEWORK.md` must consume and enforce those vetoes during decision-making and must not redefine, weaken, or override them. `SCORING_MODEL.md` may inform decisions but must never override a Risk Policy veto.

### 11.4 No false precision

Intrinsic value should generally be treated as a range rather than an exact number.

The system must identify key assumptions and distinguish:

- facts;
- estimates;
- forecasts;
- assumptions.

## 12. Market, Money Flow, and Technical Analysis

### 12.1 Secondary role

Market conditions, money-flow indicators, sentiment, and technical analysis are secondary context and execution tools. This includes, where available, foreign-investor flows, proprietary-trading flows, volume, momentum, liquidity, and other short-term market-positioning indicators.

They may be used to:

- optimize entry timing;
- avoid poor short-term execution;
- identify abnormal momentum, liquidity, or positioning conditions;
- stage purchases;
- adjust tranche sizing within approved risk limits;
- improve risk/reward at entry.

### 12.2 Market / money flow / technical indicators cannot create the thesis

Market flow, money-flow, sentiment, and technical signals must not override poor business quality, weak financial health, excessive valuation, a policy-level veto, or a broken thesis. Strong short-term flows or momentum are never sufficient reasons for BUY or ACCUMULATE.

Conversely, weak flows, foreign selling, negative sentiment, or weak technical conditions must not convert a fundamentally attractive, valuation-supported investment into AVOID solely on those grounds.

These indicators may influence **execution timing, staging, tranche sizing, or the degree of short-term caution**, but must not determine business quality, intrinsic value, thesis validity, or long-term ownership merit.

The long-term investment thesis must remain fundamentally driven.

## 13. Decision States

Every security under review must ultimately be assigned exactly one of the following states, with definitions that are unambiguous relative to whether the security is currently owned or unowned:

- **STRONG BUY**
- **BUY**
- **ACCUMULATE**
- **HOLD**
- **REDUCE**
- **SELL**
- **AVOID**

Each final decision must include:

1. Score.
2. Investment thesis.
3. Key positives.
4. Key risks.
5. Valuation assessment.
6. Suggested action.
7. Conditions that would invalidate the thesis.

The **score is decision support, not an automatic decision authority**. A high score must not override a policy-level veto, broken thesis, mandate violation, or unacceptable risk.

Detailed definitions and thresholds for these states, including owned-versus-unowned semantics, will be established in `DECISION_FRAMEWORK.md` and `SCORING_MODEL.md`.

## 14. Sell and Reduce Philosophy

A holding should be reviewed for REDUCE or SELL when one or more of the following occurs:

- the original thesis is invalidated;
- business quality materially deteriorates;
- financial risk becomes unacceptable;
- governance concerns materially increase;
- intrinsic value falls materially;
- market price becomes sufficiently excessive relative to expected value;
- position size creates unacceptable portfolio risk;
- a materially superior opportunity creates significant opportunity cost after uncertainty, thesis confidence, transaction costs, taxes where relevant, liquidity, and lost future compounding are considered;
- the security becomes inconsistent with the mandate and orderly exit is warranted.

A stock must **not** be sold solely because:

- it has risen significantly;
- it has reached a 20% gain;
- the investor wants to "lock in profit" without reference to valuation;
- a temporary technical signal turns negative while the long-term thesis remains intact.

### 14.1 Forward-return principle

HOLD, REDUCE, and SELL decisions must emphasize the **expected forward return from the current market price**, not the historical gain or loss since purchase. Past performance and cost basis are context, not primary decision anchors.

Every HOLD is implicitly a decision to continue allocating the position's current market value to that security. The system should therefore ask whether the expected forward return, thesis quality, downside risk, portfolio role, and opportunity cost remain attractive today.

This does not mean HOLD and BUY have identical hurdles: taxes, transaction costs, liquidity, switching uncertainty, and the higher hurdle for replacing a valid long-term holding must still be considered.

## 15. Behavioral Risk Policy

The system must actively flag decisions that may be influenced by behavioral bias, including:

- FOMO;
- loss aversion;
- anchoring to purchase price;
- anchoring to historical highs;
- averaging down to avoid admitting an error;
- selling winners too early;
- confirmation bias;
- recency bias;
- overconfidence;
- action bias.

When a behavioral warning is triggered, the recommendation should explicitly identify the bias and require the investment case to be restated using current facts, valuation, and opportunity cost.

## 16. Data and Evidence Standards

### 16.1 Recency

Real investment decisions must use the latest reasonably available data and explicitly identify:

- the **as-of date** of the analysis;
- the reporting period of material financial inputs;
- the date of market-price inputs where relevant;
- and any material stale-data warning.

Data that is old enough to impair decision quality must not be presented as current without an explicit limitation.

When a newer authoritative disclosure is known to exist and is reasonably accessible, older data must not be used as the primary current input without explicit explanation. If the newest authoritative data cannot be obtained, the analysis must flag the missing update and reduce confidence where the gap is decision-relevant.

### 16.2 Data hierarchy

Where available, preference should be given to:

1. audited or official company disclosures;
2. exchange/regulatory disclosures;
3. official index data;
4. reputable financial data providers;
5. broker/analyst research as secondary interpretation;
6. market commentary and community sentiment as supplementary context only.

### 16.3 No fabricated data

Missing information must be identified as missing.

The system must not invent financial values, prices, forecasts, index composition, ownership data, or valuation inputs.

### 16.4 Fact / estimate / assumption separation

Material analytical claims should be classified where appropriate as:

- **FACT**
- **ESTIMATE**
- **ASSUMPTION**

## 17. Review Cadence Principles

Detailed workflows will be defined later, but at minimum the system should support:

- event-driven review after material company news;
- review when the VN30 composition changes;
- review before adding to an existing position;
- review after material valuation changes;
- periodic portfolio-level risk review;
- periodic review of investment decisions and behavioral errors.

Frequent price checking must not be confused with fundamental review.

## 18. Governance and Policy Hierarchy

### 18.1 Policy hierarchy

The documents of the VN30 Value Investing OS should operate in the following hierarchy:

1. `INVESTMENT_POLICY.md`
2. `RISK_POLICY.md`
3. `DECISION_FRAMEWORK.md`
4. `SCORING_MODEL.md`
5. Operating workflows, implementation logic, and automation

Lower-level rules must not contradict higher-level policies.

### 18.2 Policy changes

Material changes to investment universe, use of leverage, return objective, drawdown philosophy, investment horizon, or core investment style require explicit policy review rather than silent modification by the system.

### 18.3 Exceptions

Exceptions should be rare, explicit, documented, and reviewed. Every exception must record the rationale, approval authority, effective date, and expiry or mandatory review date.

No exception may permit:

- margin or leverage;
- intentional purchase of an ineligible security;
- fabricated data;
- bypassing thesis review for averaging down.

## 19. Explicit Prohibitions

The system must not:

- use margin or leverage;
- purchase securities outside the eligible VN30 universe;
- treat low P/E as sufficient evidence of undervaluation;
- buy solely because price has fallen;
- sell solely because price has risen;
- average down solely to reduce cost basis;
- target break-even as an investment thesis;
- force monthly deployment when opportunities are unattractive;
- ignore minimum trading-lot constraints;
- ignore portfolio concentration or correlated exposures;
- fabricate missing market or company data;
- allow technical analysis to override a fundamentally broken thesis.

---

## 20. Success Criteria for This Policy

The policy is successful if it causes the system to:

- remain within the VN30 mandate;
- favor long-term business ownership over short-term trading;
- deploy capital selectively rather than mechanically;
- separate price movement from business value;
- avoid leverage;
- prevent mechanical averaging down;
- manage concentration and downside risk consciously;
- retain strong businesses when forward returns remain attractive;
- document why each investment decision is made;
- and improve decisions through review rather than outcome-based hindsight.

## 23. Key Rules Summary — Version 1.1

The following rules are the highest-priority operating principles of this Investment Policy:

1. **VN30-only mandate.** New purchases are limited to current VN30 constituents. A removed constituent becomes a Legacy Holding, cannot receive new capital, and must follow a documented orderly exit plan.
2. **Value first, not cheapness first.** Low P/E, falling price, or historical discount is never sufficient evidence of value.
3. **Stage 0 comes before valuation.** Eligibility, minimum investability, reporting reliability, and Risk Policy vetoes must be screened before valuation can influence a positive capital-allocation decision.
4. **Risk Policy owns vetoes.** Non-compensable risks can veto an investment regardless of valuation. `RISK_POLICY.md` defines hard vetoes; the Decision Framework enforces them; scoring cannot override them.
5. **Capital preservation precedes return chasing.** The 15%–20% long-term total-return CAGR target is aspirational and subordinate to mandate compliance, avoidance of unacceptable permanent-loss risk, and portfolio resilience.
6. **No leverage.** Margin and borrowing for investment purposes are prohibited.
7. **DCA contributions do not require DCA deployment.** Cash may accumulate when opportunities or risk constraints justify it, but speculative short-term market timing must not be the primary reason for holding unusually high cash.
8. **Every Add is fresh underwriting.** Additional capital requires an updated thesis, valuation, risk review, expected return, portfolio-impact review, and opportunity-cost comparison.
9. **Never average down to repair cost basis or reach break-even.** Purchase price is not intrinsic value.
10. **20% drawdown is a soft escalation threshold, not a stop-loss.** Drawdown must trigger diagnosis of its source; market beta and permanent business impairment must be treated differently.
11. **Holding periods are long-term by default.** A 20% gain is only a review trigger, never an automatic profit-taking rule.
12. **Switching requires a materially superior alternative.** Marginal score differences are insufficient to justify selling a valid compounder.
13. **Valuation uses a primary sector-appropriate method plus relevant cross-checks.** More models do not automatically mean better analysis.
14. **Market flow, money flow, sentiment, and technical analysis are secondary.** They may optimize execution and short-term caution but cannot create, override, or invalidate the fundamental thesis by themselves.
15. **Scores do not override vetoes or judgment.** A high score cannot bypass mandate violations, unacceptable risks, or a broken thesis.
16. **Decision States must be ownership-aware.** STRONG BUY / BUY / ACCUMULATE / HOLD / REDUCE / SELL / AVOID must have precise meanings for owned and unowned securities in `DECISION_FRAMEWORK.md`.
17. **Use the newest authoritative data available.** Every real analysis must show an as-of date and material data dates; known newer authoritative disclosures cannot be bypassed without explanation and a confidence warning.
18. **Exceptions are controlled.** Any exception requires documented rationale, approval authority, effective date, and expiry or mandatory review date.
19. **Cash and benchmarks must not distort the mandate.** Cash is an active alternative to unattractive opportunities, not a short-term market-timing bet; VN30 benchmark comparison is for evaluation, not closet indexing or tracking-error control.
20. **Forward return governs continued ownership.** HOLD / REDUCE / SELL decisions emphasize expected return from today's price, thesis quality, downside risk, and opportunity cost—not historical gain, loss, or cost basis.
